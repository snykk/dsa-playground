## What a Fibonacci heap is

A binary heap is one tree stored in an array and kept tidy after every operation. A Fibonacci heap does the opposite: it keeps a **collection of min-heap-ordered trees** and tidies up only when it has to.

- The roots of the trees sit in a **circular doubly linked list**, the root list.
- Every node stores its key, its `degree` (number of children), a `parent` pointer, one `child` pointer and the `left` and `right` links of its sibling ring.
- A single pointer, `min_node`, points at the root with the smallest key. Every non-root key is at least its parent key, so that root is the global minimum.

<Callout title="Laziness is the design">
Insert does no comparisons with the rest of the heap and no tree merging. It just splices a one-node tree into the root list. The cost of the mess is paid later by extract-min, which cleans up all the trees in one sweep.
</Callout>

<Callout tone="warning" title="What this repository does not have">
The textbook Fibonacci heap also offers `decrease_key`, `delete` and `merge` (union of two heaps in O(1)). **None of the three implementations here has them**, and the `marked` flag on each node is stored but never used. What the code provides is the lazy core: a binomial-style heap with O(1) insert and consolidation on extract-min. Treat the decrease-key discussion below as theory, not as an API you can call.
</Callout>

## Extract min and consolidation

`extract_min()` is the only operation that restructures anything:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Promote the children">
    Every child of `min_node` loses its parent pointer and joins the root list.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Unlink the minimum">
    Remove it from the root list and free it. If it was the only root and had no children, the heap is now empty.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Consolidate">
    Walk the root list once. Keep a table indexed by degree. When two trees of the same degree `d` meet, make the one with the larger root a child of the other: the result has degree `d + 1`. Repeat until every degree appears at most once.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find the new minimum">
    Scan the (now short) root list for the smallest root.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Inserting `5, 3, 7, 1, 9, 2, 8` leaves seven separate roots, with `1` as the minimum. Extracting it:

Extracting `2` next promotes its children `8` and `7 (9)`, giving roots `3 (5)`, `8` and `7 (9)`. Consolidation links `3` and `7`, both of degree 1, into one tree `3 (5, 7 (9))` of degree 2. The roots are now `3` and `8`.

## Why it is O(log n) amortized

Consolidation can look expensive: one extract-min may touch many roots. The accounting argument uses a potential function:

<Equation label="Potential" expr="Phi = t + 2m" result="t = number of trees, m = number of marked nodes" />

Insert raises `t` by one, so it costs O(1) plus one unit of potential. Consolidation spends one unit per tree it links, and every link lowers `t` by one, so the work is paid for by potential saved earlier. What remains is the cost proportional to the **maximum degree** `D(n)`, the number of distinct degrees left after consolidation.

The bound on `D(n)` comes from the shape of trees. In a full Fibonacci heap (with cascading cuts) a node of degree `d` has a subtree of at least `F(d+2)` nodes, where `F` is the Fibonacci sequence, which is where the name comes from. Since `F(d+2)` grows like `1.618^d`, we get `D(n) = O(log n)`. The repository's variant never cuts nodes, so its trees are binomial trees, in which a tree of degree `d` has exactly `2^d` nodes. The degree bound `D(n) <= log2(n)` therefore holds there too.

## Decrease-key (theory only here)

Decrease-key is the reason Fibonacci heaps exist. The node is cut from its parent and moved to the root list in O(1). To keep the degree bound, a node that loses a second child is cut too (a *cascading cut*), which is what the `marked` flag records. The cuts are paid for by the `2m` term of the potential, giving **O(1) amortized**.

That is what improves Dijkstra and Prim from O(E log V) with a binary heap to O(E + V log V): they call decrease-key up to E times but extract-min only V times.

## Applications

- **Dijkstra and Prim** with many decrease-key calls on dense graphs: the reason the structure was invented. The asymptotic gain needs a version with `decrease_key`, which this repository does not include.
- **Teaching amortized analysis**: the potential-function argument is a classic example.
- **Insert-heavy priority queues** where most elements are never extracted: the lazy insert costs almost nothing.

## Common mistakes

<Callout tone="warning" title="Watch out for these">
- **Believing the insert advantage is the point.** A binary heap also has O(1) average insert. The advantage is decrease-key.
- **Calling O(log n) extract-min a worst-case bound.** It is amortized; one call can cost O(n).
- **Forgetting to clear the parent pointer** when promoting children to the root list.
- **Iterating the root list while modifying it.** Consolidation first copies the roots into a list, then links them.
- **Copying a heap in C++.** Nodes are owned through raw pointers, so the C++ class has its copy constructor and assignment deleted.
- **Reading from an empty heap.** `peek` and `extract_min` raise `IndexError` in Python, throw `std::runtime_error` in C++ and panic in Go.
</Callout>

## Implementation

All three versions keep `min_node`, the circular root list, a degree table for consolidation and a `count`. Python and Go hold the degree table in a map, C++ in a `std::map`.

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
  <FaqItem question="Why is it called a Fibonacci heap?">
    In the full structure, a node of degree d has at least F(d+2) descendants including itself, where F is the Fibonacci sequence. That growth bound is what keeps the maximum degree logarithmic.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Does this repository implement decrease-key?">
    No. Only insert, extract-min, peek, size, is-empty and clear exist, and the `marked` field is unused. It is the lazy consolidating core.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Is it faster than a binary heap?">
    Asymptotically better for decrease-key-heavy workloads, but the large constants and poor locality mean binary heaps usually win on real inputs.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Why does extract-min consolidate?">
    Without it the root list would only grow, and finding the next minimum would take O(n). Linking equal-degree trees leaves at most one tree per degree, so the root list has O(log n) entries.
  </FaqItem>
</Faq>
