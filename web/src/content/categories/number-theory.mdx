## The problem

These topics answer questions about whole numbers without floating point: what divides these two numbers, what is a power modulo `m`, which numbers are prime, and which number has these remainders? All results are exact, and most are fast because they work on remainders instead of full values.

The topics form a chain:

- The **Euclidean algorithm** finds gcd and, in its extended form, the inverse of a number modulo `m`.
- **Modular arithmetic** keeps numbers small with `mod` and divides with that inverse.
- **Modular exponentiation** computes huge powers inside that arithmetic.
- The **Chinese Remainder Theorem** merges congruences, with one extended Euclid per step.
- The **sieve** and **Miller-Rabin** are two ways to find primes: all of them up to `n`, or a verdict on one large number. Miller-Rabin is built on modular exponentiation.

## Applications

- **Cryptography.** Modular exponentiation and Miller-Rabin generate and use RSA-size keys.
- **Competitive programming.** Answers modulo a prime, inverses for division, and sieves for factor tables.
- **Hashing.** Rolling hashes compute in modular arithmetic.
- **Scheduling and cycles.** The Chinese Remainder Theorem finds when several periodic events line up.

## Common mistakes

<Callout tone="warning" title="Watch out for these">
- **Computing `a * b / gcd` for the lcm.** The product can overflow. Divide first: `a / gcd * b`.
- **Assuming an inverse always exists.** It needs `gcd(a, m) = 1`. Python's `mod_inverse` returns `None` otherwise.
- **Trusting a remainder of a negative number.** The results here always land in `0 .. m-1`, but some languages return a negative remainder for a negative operand.
- **Overflowing 64-bit products.** Modular multiplication of two values near `m` needs 128-bit intermediates once `m` is above about 3.04e9, in both exponentiation and Miller-Rabin.
- **Using a sieve for one big number.** It needs O(n) memory and is only practical for n up to a few hundred million. Use Miller-Rabin instead.
- **Forgetting that Fermat's test is fooled.** Carmichael numbers such as 561 pass it for every coprime base, which is why Miller-Rabin tests more than `a^(n-1) = 1`.
</Callout>

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
  <FaqItem question="In what order should I read these?">
    Euclidean algorithm, modular arithmetic, modular exponentiation, the Chinese Remainder Theorem, the sieve, then Miller-Rabin. Each of the first four leans on the one before it, and Miller-Rabin needs modular exponentiation.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Do the moduli have to be coprime for the Chinese Remainder Theorem?">
    No. The implementation merges congruences one at a time and reports no solution when two of them contradict each other. With coprime moduli a solution always exists and is unique modulo the product.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Is Miller-Rabin exact?">
    With random witnesses it is probabilistic, and a composite number slips past one witness with probability at most 1/4. The deterministic version with 12 fixed bases is exact for every 64-bit integer. A prime is never rejected.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Why does modular exponentiation need so few multiplications?">
    It squares the base once per bit of the exponent and multiplies the answer in only for the 1 bits, so a power with an exponent near 10^18 takes about 120 multiplications.
  </FaqItem>
</Faq>
