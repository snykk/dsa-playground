## The problem

A queue holds items that wait their turn. Items go in at one end and leave by a rule. The plain rule is first in, first out (FIFO): the item that has waited longest leaves first.

The four structures in this section answer three different questions:

- **Which item leaves next?** The oldest (simple and circular queue), the one with the best priority (priority queue), or whichever end you ask for (deque).
- **How is it stored?** In a growable array, in a fixed ring, or in a heap.
- **What does removing the front cost?** O(1), O(n) or O(log n), depending on the answers above.

The circular queue is the only bounded one: `enqueue` on a full queue raises `OverflowError` in Python, and the capacity must be at least 1. Peek is O(1) in all four.

## See them side by side

The simple and the circular queue share their input box, so one script can run on both: the simple queue shifts every item on a dequeue, while the circular queue only moves `front`. The deque and the priority queue take their own operations.

## One sequence, four queues

Add `A`, `B` and `C`, then remove twice. The priority queue gets the priorities 2, 1 and 2. The circular queue has capacity 3, and the deque adds with `push_back`, then uses `pop_front` followed by `pop_back`. These results come from running the Python versions.

    ['Deque (`pop_front`, then `pop_back`)', '`A`', '`C`', '`B`'],
    ['Priority queue (A: 2, B: 1, C: 2)', '`B`', '`A`', '`C`'],
  ]}
/>

The simple and circular queue always agree. The deque removes from the end you pick, so its second removal is `C`. The priority queue serves `B` first because 1 is the best priority, and then `A` before `C`, because equal priorities leave in the order they arrived.

## Applications

- **Breadth-first search.** The queue holds the frontier in discovery order.
- **Scheduling.** Round-robin task queues, print queues and request buffers.
- **Sliding window and monotonic queue problems.** A deque keeps candidates at both ends.
- **Shortest paths and event simulation.** A priority queue picks the next vertex or the next event.

## Common mistakes

<Callout tone="warning" title="Watch out for these">
- **Assuming dequeue is O(1) in the simple queue.** In Python and C++ it shifts every remaining item. Only Go reslices.
- **Using the deque's front as the fast end.** It is array-backed here, so `push_front` is O(n) in every language.
- **Enqueueing on a full circular queue.** It raises `OverflowError` in Python instead of growing.
- **Expecting a highest-first priority queue.** The lowest value leaves first. For max behaviour, negate the priorities.
- **Expecting sorted iteration from the priority queue.** Only the next item is guaranteed; the heap array is not in sorted order.
</Callout>

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
  <FaqItem question="In what order should I read these?">
    Start with the simple queue, because the circular queue and the deque each fix one of its weaknesses. Then the circular queue, the deque and the priority queue. The priority queue also needs the min heap, from the Heaps section.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Why does the circular queue exist if the simple queue works?">
    Dequeue on the simple queue costs O(n) in Python and C++. The circular queue moves two indices instead of the items, so both operations are O(1), at the price of a fixed capacity.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="When do equal priorities matter?">
    The priority queue stores an insertion counter with each item, so two items with the same priority leave in the order they were added. Without it the order among ties would be arbitrary.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Is a deque a queue or a stack?">
    Both. Pushing and popping at the back gives a stack. Pushing at the back and popping at the front gives a queue.
  </FaqItem>
</Faq>
