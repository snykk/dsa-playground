## The problem

A new problem rarely looks like one you have solved. What carries over is its shape: a sorted array, a contiguous range, many queries on fixed data, a yes/no question that flips once. Each pattern in this section names a shape and the saving that comes with it, usually one nested loop removed.

The patterns fall into four groups:

- **Scans over a sequence:** two pointers, sliding window, and the monotonic stack and queue. They run in O(n) because each index moves forward and is never revisited, or is pushed and popped once.
- **Precomputed structure:** prefix sums and difference arrays, and coordinate compression. They spend one pass up front so that later steps are O(1) or O(log n).
- **Search and counting:** binary search on the answer, DP patterns, inclusion-exclusion, and meet in the middle. They cut the search space instead of walking it.
- **Structure of the problem:** union-find, graph traversal, and bit manipulation. They change what you represent, such as groups, a graph or a subset in one integer.

The skill is to read the statement for the signal first and pick the pattern second.

## Choosing a pattern

Several patterns can fit one problem, and they often combine. Binary search on the answer uses a greedy check, and sometimes a two-pointer check inside it. The Monotonic Queue is a sliding window with a faster maximum.

## Applications

- **Interviews and contests.** Most array, string and graph problems reduce to one of these shapes.
- **Streaming and monitoring.** A sliding window with a monotonic queue tracks a rolling maximum over the last k readings.
- **Offline analytics.** Prefix sums and difference arrays answer or apply thousands of range operations in one pass.
- **Networks and clustering.** Union-find tracks connected components as links appear.

## Common mistakes

<Callout tone="warning" title="Watch out for these">
- **Forcing a pattern without its precondition.** Two pointers from both ends need a sorted or monotone array, a sliding window needs monotone validity, and binary search on the answer needs a check that flips once.
- **Using prefix sums when updates and queries mix.** They are static or offline tools. Use a Fenwick tree or a segment tree for online updates.
- **Treating union-find as reversible.** It only merges. It cannot split a group or delete an edge.
- **Using BFS on a weighted graph.** BFS counts edges, not weights, so the distances it reports are wrong when edges differ in cost.
- **Guessing the cost of 2^n.** Bitmask enumeration is practical for about 20 to 25 items, and meet in the middle stretches that to about 30 to 45. Beyond that, look for another formulation.
- **Mixing up the two monotonic structures.** The stack has no front eviction and answers neighbour questions. The queue evicts from the front when an index leaves the window.
</Callout>

## Frequently asked questions

<Faq>
  <FaqItem question="In what order should I learn them?">
    Start with two pointers, sliding window and prefix sums: they are the Beginner topics and they appear in a large share of array and string problems. Then take binary search on the answer, the monotonic stack and queue, union-find and graph traversal. Bit manipulation, coordinate compression, DP patterns and inclusion-exclusion come next, and meet in the middle comes last.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="How do I tell two pointers from a sliding window?">
    Two pointers usually move toward each other on a sorted array and decide which end to drop from a comparison. A sliding window keeps a contiguous range and moves both ends forward, growing and shrinking it until a constraint holds. A window answers questions about subarrays and substrings, and opposite-end pointers answer questions about pairs.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Which patterns need a sorted array?">
    Opposite-end two pointers do, and so does any binary search over values. The sliding window, the monotonic stack and queue, and prefix sums work on an array in any order.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Do these replace dynamic programming and greedy algorithms?">
    No. They sit beside them. Binary search on the answer pairs with a greedy check, the DP patterns topic is the recipe for the DP family, and inclusion-exclusion and bitmasks often feed a DP. The Algorithms folder holds the full DP and greedy topics.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="What if no pattern fits?">
    Write the brute force, find the repeated work in it, and ask which pattern removes that repetition. A repeated range sum points to prefix sums, a repeated scan to the next greater element points to a monotonic stack, and a repeated subproblem points to DP.
  </FaqItem>
</Faq>
